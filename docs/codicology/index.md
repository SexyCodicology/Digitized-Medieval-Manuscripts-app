# What is Codicology? And what about Palaeography?

!!! info "A growing primer"
    This section introduces a few codicology and palaeography topics, not an
    exhaustive reference. If there's a script or manuscript type you'd like
    to see covered, [open an issue](https://github.com/SexyCodicology/Digitized-Medieval-Manuscripts-app/issues).

## What does "Codicology" mean?

The origins of the term "Codicology" can be traced back to Alphonse Dain, a French scholar of Byzantine and Hellenic studies, who first coined the term in his seminal work "Les manuscrits" in 1949. Dain, recognizing the need for a single word to describe the study of manuscripts in English, noted that the Germans already had a word for it, namely "Handschriftenkunde". Consequently, Dain proposed "Codicology" as a neologism by combining two words: "codex" or "codicis" in Latin, meaning "book(s)", and "-logia" in Greek, meaning "the study of a certain subject". Hence, the word "Codicology" signifies the study of books, particularly manuscripts.

However, it is important to note that Codicology is a vast field of study, encompassing many different areas of research. Thus, while the term may seem all-encompassing, it is not without its limitations. For instance, it does not address the specific study of ancient writings, which is the domain of "Palaeography". Palaeography, which also derives from Greek roots "palaiós" and "graphein", meaning "old-write", is the study of ancient writings, including those found in manuscripts (think of [Caroline Minuscule](medieval-scripts/caroline-minuscule.md), [Humanistic Script](medieval-scripts/humanistic-script.md), [Beneventan Script](medieval-scripts/beneventan-script.md), etc.). As such, it is a distinct but complementary field to Codicology, with its own set of methodologies, terminologies, and theories.

## Codicology vs Palaeography

You might be wondering why these two words appear on this page together. Well, they are both equally important when studying manuscripts. They are often used to indicate the same thing. So let's give a look:

- **Codicology focuses on the physical aspects of the book.** Observing the details of a manuscript, a codicologist is able to learn more about its origins, provenance, and its role in the life of who owned it, dating, etc. (Hunter, Timothy. "Codicology." *The Oxford Companion to Western Art*. Oxford University Press, 2001.) It essentially takes the path of looking at the "big things to the small things" in order to better understand a book.
- **Palaeography focuses on the history of the scripts and the handwriting.** Analyzing a manuscript, a palaeographer is also able to discover the place of origin of the book, dating, etc. (Beal, Peter. "palaeography." *A Dictionary of English Manuscript Terminology 1450–2000*. Oxford University Press, 2008.) Here the path is instead inverted: first we look at something very specific (the style in which the words are written) and from there we work our way to the bigger picture, in order to understand what a book is trying to tell us.

These definitions are exactly what makes choosing between codicology and palaeography complex. Both reach the same goal, but in different ways. The goal is always the same: understanding a manuscript and its origins better. Some researchers are of the opinion that codicology is part of palaeography, others believe it's the other way around, with codicology being the better word (Beal, Peter. "codicology." *A Dictionary of English Manuscript Terminology 1450–2000*. Oxford University Press, 2008.).

The truth is that there is no single word that really encompasses the whole wonderful universe that surrounds a medieval book, and that's OK. Both are fantastic and, depending on how you proceed to study a manuscript, and your expertise, you can be a palaeographer or a codicologist, and you should always seek the advice of someone who might know more about the details you don't.

For example: a palaeographer would be able to say that manuscript Hatton MS 116, Oxford, Bodleian Library, was very possibly at Worcester at a certain point, since the glosses in the manuscript are written by the legendary [Tremulous Hand of Worcester](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Tremulous_Hand_of_Worcester). A codicologist might arrive at the same conclusion by analyzing the parchment, the binding, the layout, etc. Both are excellent research methods.
